⌁ Three methods to master from HW-13
1. Sum function (§13.7): start from \(\sum x^n=\frac{1}{1-x}\); differentiate to bring down factors of \(n\), or integrate to divide by \(n\); match your series, then fix the constant with one known value. 2. Expand at \(x_0\) (§13.8): substitute into a known series (\(e^u,\sin u,\frac{1}{1-u},\ln(1+u)\)); for rational functions use partial fractions; for a center \(x_0\neq0\) rewrite everything in \((x-x_0)\). 3. Fourier → numerical sum (§13.11): build the Fourier series, then plug in a clever \(x\) (at a jump, use the midpoint) to read off the numerical series. Also: when the ratio test gives \(1\), try Raabe (§13.4): \(R=\lim n\!\left(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)\); \(R>1\) converges, \(R<1\) diverges.
§13.7 — Sum function of a power series
§13.7 (1)worked example
Find the sum function of \(\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{x^n}{n(n-1)}\) and its interval.
Method: one differentiation removes \(1/(n(n-1))\) down to \(1/(n-1)\), giving a known log; then integrate back.
Worked solutionStep 1 — differentiate. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{x^n}{n(n-1)}=\dfrac{x^{n-1}}{n-1}\). So \(f\'(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{x^{n-1}}{n-1}\xlongequal{m=n-1}\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{x^m}{m}=-\ln(1-x).\)
Step 2 — integrate back. \(f(x)=\displaystyle\int -\ln(1-x)\,dx=(1-x)\ln(1-x)+x+C\) (since \(\int\ln(1-x)\,dx=(x-1)\ln(1-x)-x\)).
Step 3 — fix \(C\). The series starts at \(n=2\), so \(f(0)=0\). Plugging \(x=0\): \((1)\ln 1+0+C=C\Rightarrow C=0\).
Step 4 — interval. Ratio test \(\Rightarrow R=1\); at \(x=\pm1\), \(\sum\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\) converges (telescopes).
\(f(x)=x+(1-x)\ln(1-x)\), on \([-1,1]\).
§13.7 (5)worked example
Find the sum function of \(\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\,x^{n-1}\).
Method: recognize it as a second derivative of the geometric series — i.e. the binomial series \(1/(1-x)^3\).
Worked solutionStep 1 — known series. \(\dfrac{1}{1-x}=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}x^n\). Differentiate once: \(\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{n\ge1}n x^{n-1}\). Differentiate again: \(\dfrac{2}{(1-x)^3}=\sum_{n\ge2}n(n-1)x^{n-2}\).
Step 2 — reindex to \(1/(1-x)^3\). \(\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^3}=\displaystyle\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}x^{m}\).
Step 3 — match. Our series with \(m=n-1\): \(\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}=\dfrac{(m+1)(m+2)}{2}\) and \(x^{n-1}=x^{m}\). So it is \(\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^3}\).
\(=\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^3}\), \(|x|<1\).
§13.8 — Expand a function into a power series at \(x_0\)
§13.8 (5)worked example
Expand \(\sin(x^2)\) at \(x_0=0\); give the radius.
Method: never differentiate repeatedly — just substitute into the known \(\sin\) series.
Worked solutionKnown: \(\sin u=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n u^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\) for all \(u\).
Substitute \(u=x^2\): \(\sin(x^2)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n (x^2)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{4n+2}}{(2n+1)!}.\)
\(\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{4n+2}}{(2n+1)!}\), \(R=\infty\).
§13.8 (9)worked example
Expand \(\dfrac{x}{(x-1)(x+3)}\) at \(x_0=0\).
Method: partial fractions, then turn each piece into a geometric series.
Worked solutionStep 1 — partial fractions. \(\dfrac{x}{(x-1)(x+3)}=\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{B}{x+3}\); \(A(x+3)+B(x-1)=x\). \(x=1\Rightarrow A=\tfrac14\); \(x=-3\Rightarrow B=\tfrac34\).
Step 2 — geometric each. \(\tfrac14\cdot\dfrac{1}{x-1}=-\tfrac14\dfrac{1}{1-x}=-\tfrac14\sum x^n\) \((|x|<1)\); \(\tfrac34\cdot\dfrac{1}{x+3}=\tfrac14\dfrac{1}{1+x/3}=\tfrac14\sum\left(-\tfrac{x}{3}\right)^n\) \((|x|<3)\).
Step 3 — combine. \(f(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\Big[-\tfrac14+\tfrac14\big(-\tfrac13\big)^n\Big]x^n=\frac14\sum_{n\ge0}\Big[\big(-\tfrac13\big)^n-1\Big]x^n.\) The binding radius is the smaller, \(R=1\).
\(\frac14\sum\big[(-\tfrac13)^n-1\big]x^n\), \(|x|<1\).
§13.8 (6)worked example
Expand \(\ln(3-x)\) at \(x_0=-1\); give the interval.
Method: shift to the new center: write everything in \(u=x-x_0=x+1\), then use the \(\ln(1+u)\) series.
Worked solutionStep 1 — shift. Let \(u=x+1\). Then \(3-x=4-u=4\big(1-\tfrac{u}{4}\big)\).
Step 2 — log series. \(\ln\!\big[4(1-\tfrac{u}{4})\big]=\ln4+\ln\!\big(1-\tfrac{u}{4}\big)=\ln4-\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(u/4)^n}{n}=\ln4-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(x+1)^n}{n\,4^n}.\)
Step 3 — interval. \(\big|\tfrac{u}{4}\big|<1\Rightarrow|x+1|<4\Rightarrow-5<x<3\). At \(x=3\) (\(u=4\)): \(-\sum\tfrac1n\) diverges; at \(x=-5\) (\(u=-4\)): \(-\sum\tfrac{(-1)^n}{n}\) converges.
\(\ln4-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(x+1)^n}{n\,4^n}\), on \([-5,3)\).
§13.11 — Use a Fourier series to sum a numerical series
§13.11 (via \(x^2\))worked example
Using the Fourier series of \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-\pi,\pi]\), find \(\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2}\) and \(\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}\).
Method: build the Fourier series, then plug in two clever values of \(x\) and read off the sums.
Worked solutionStep 1 — Fourier series. \(f\) is even, so \(b_n=0\); \(a_0=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2dx=\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}\), \(a_n=\tfrac1\pi\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2\cos(nx)\,dx=\tfrac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\). So \(x^2=\dfrac{\pi^2}{3}+\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos(nx).\)
Step 2 — plug \(x=\pi\) (\(f\) is continuous there, \(f(\pi)=\pi^2\)): \(\pi^2=\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}+\sum\tfrac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}(-1)^n=\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}+4\sum\tfrac1{n^2}\Rightarrow\boxed{\sum\tfrac1{n^2}=\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}}.\)
Step 3 — plug \(x=0\) (\(f(0)=0\)): \(0=\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}+\sum\tfrac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\Rightarrow\sum\tfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2}=-\tfrac{\pi^2}{12}\Rightarrow\boxed{\sum\tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\tfrac{\pi^2}{12}}.\)
\(\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{12}\).

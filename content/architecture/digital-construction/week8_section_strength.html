Keep one fact in mind: the neutral axis passes through the centroid. You derived it last week from ∫σ dA = 0. So the centroid is not just a geometry exercise. It decides how far the top and bottom fibres are from the axis, and therefore which one is stressed most.

Part 2

Static moment (静矩): the balance of an area

Sz = ∫ y dA = A · yc

Cut the area into tiny pieces, multiply each by its signed distance y from the axis, and add. For a shape with a known centroid, you don't need the integral: it is the area × the distance from the axis to that area's own centroid.

Think of the section as a flat plate on a knife edge along the axis. Sz measures how much it wants to tip. Three consequences:

  • Sz has a sign. Area on the positive-y side counts +, area on the negative side counts −. Units are length³.
  • The centroid axis is where the plate balances, so Sz about any centroidal axis is exactly 0.
  • Cut a section along a centroidal axis into two parts. Their static moments must cancel: equal size, opposite sign, however different the two parts look.

The two parts of a section on either side of its centroid axis have different shapes, but equal and opposite static moments.

Worked example A rectangle 100 mm wide and 60 mm tall. About its base: S = (100 × 60) × 30 = 180 000 mm³. About its own mid-height axis: the top half gives (100 × 30) × (+15) = +45 000 and the bottom half gives (100 × 30) × (−15) = −45 000, so the total is 0 ✓.

The part-section static moment comes back in shear stress: Sz* in τ = FsSz*/(Izb) is exactly this kind of quantity.

Sign depends on the axis direction If y points down (as in the figure for homework Problem 3), the signs of every part flip. The fact that the two parts are opposite does not change.
Part 3

Finding the centroid of a composite section

yc = Σ Ai yi / Σ Ai
  1. If the section has a line of symmetry, the centroid lies on it. Only one coordinate is left to find.
  2. Split the section into rectangles or triangles whose centroids you know.
  3. Pick one reference line (the bottom edge is easiest) and measure every yi from it.
  4. Add up. Treat a hole as a negative area.
Worked example A narrow block 40 wide × 60 tall sits on a base plate 100 wide × 20 tall (mm). Base plate: A = 2000, y = 10. Block: A = 2400, y = 20 + 30 = 50. So yc = (2000 × 10 + 2400 × 50)/4400 = 140 000/4400 = 31.8 mm from the bottom. Sanity check: it must lie between 10 and 50, and closer to the larger area.
Common slip yi is measured to the centroid of each part, from the common reference line. Forgetting to add the 20 mm base plate height to the block's 30 mm is the most common mistake.
Part 4

Moment of inertia (惯性矩) by strips and by shifting

Iz = ∫ y² dA      Iz = Ic + a²·A

Because y is squared, I is never negative and it rewards area placed far from the axis. To find it for a new shape, slice the shape into thin horizontal strips parallel to the axis: dA = (strip width) × dy.

Worked example: rectangle about its base Every strip has width b, so Ibase = ∫₀h y²·b dy = b·[y³/3]₀h = bh³/3. Check by shifting from the centroid: bh³/12 + (h/2)²·bh = bh³/12 + 3bh³/12 = bh³/3 ✓.

Sliding strips changes nothing

I depends only on how much area sits at each height. Slide a strip sideways and its y and its area stay the same. So a slanted parallelogram has the same I about a horizontal axis as a rectangle with the same base and height.

Same strip widths at the same heights, so the same I about any horizontal axis.

The parallel-axis trap I = Ic + a²A only works when one of the two axes passes through the centroid. To go between two non-centroidal axes, go through the centroid: first subtract to reach Ic, then add to reach the new axis. The centroidal value is always the smallest I among parallel axes.
Part 5

Bending stress, quickly

σ = M·y / Iz

Stress is 0 on the neutral axis and grows linearly to the outermost fibre. Sagging M has tension at the bottom. Hogging M has tension at the top.

Units: use N·mm, mm and mm⁴, and the answer is in N/mm² = MPa. So 1 kN·m = 10⁶ N·mm.

Worked example M = 20 kN·m, Iz = 100 × 10⁶ mm⁴, and a point 80 mm from the neutral axis: σ = 20×10⁶ × 80 / (100×10⁶) = 16 MPa.
Part 6

Unsymmetric sections: four numbers, not one

When the neutral axis is not at mid-height, the top and bottom fibres are at different distances. If the material also has different limits in tension and compression (cast iron and concrete are much weaker in tension), one σmax isn't enough.

The same inverted T under the two signs of moment. The far fibre always has the larger stress, but what it carries flips between compression and tension.

The procedure: take the largest sagging moment and the largest hogging moment. At each one, find which fibre is in tension and which in compression. That gives four stresses to compare with the two limits.

Worked example Iz = 10 × 10⁶ mm⁴, ytop = 100, ybottom = 40. Moments: +5 kN·m and −4 kN·m.
SectionTensionCompression
Sagging, M = 5bottom: 5×10⁶ × 40/10×10⁶ = 20 MPatop: 5×10⁶ × 100/10×10⁶ = 50 MPa
Hogging, M = 4top: 4×10⁶ × 100/10×10⁶ = 40 MPabottom: 4×10⁶ × 40/10×10⁶ = 16 MPa
The largest tension is at the smaller moment, because there the tension is on the far fibre.
Part 7

Three kinds of strength problem, and the section modulus

TaskYou knowYou find
Check (校核)loads, section, limitsIs σmax ≤ [σ]?
Design (设计截面)loads, limitsWz ≥ Mmax/[σ], then the dimensions
Allowable load (许用荷载)section, limitsMmax ≤ [σ]·Wz, then the load

For a rectangle, W = bh²/6, where h is the dimension in the direction of bending. Depth is squared, so turning a plank on edge helps a lot, even though the area doesn't change.

Worked example A 50 × 150 mm plank. On edge: W = 50 × 150²/6 = 187 500 mm³. Flat: W = 150 × 50²/6 = 62 500 mm³. On edge is 3 times stronger, and 3 is the ratio of the long side to the short side.

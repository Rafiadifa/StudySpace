Read first
The basics: how we got here from calculus

Chapter 3 gave you rules like "ΔM = area under Fs". Chapter 4 gives you σ = My/Iz. None of these is a new law. Each one is just equilibrium (ΣY = 0, ΣM = 0) applied to a tiny piece, followed by calculus. If you see where they come from, you don't have to memorise them.

1 · A load is a density, so totals are integrals

q(x) is measured in kN/m: force per metre. A tiny piece dx carries force q·dx. Add up all the pieces:

total force  Q = ∫ q dx = area of the load diagram
where it acts  x̄ = ∫ x·q dx ÷ ∫ q dx = centroid of that area

That's why a uniform load ql acts at the middle, and a triangle q0l/2 acts l/3 from the big end. You only use the resultant for reactions. Inside the beam you still need the real q(x).

2 · Cut out a slice dx and balance it

ΣY = 0: Fs + q·dx − (Fs + dFs) = 0, so

dFs/dx = q

ΣM about the right face = 0: −M − Fs·dx − q·dx·(dx/2) + (M + dM) = 0. Divide by dx: dM/dx = Fs + q·dx/2. Now the calculus step: let dx → 0. The last term vanishes (it's "second-order small", dx²), so

dM/dx = Fs   and so   d²M/dx² = q

3 · Integrate back: the diagrams are antiderivatives

Fs(x) = Fs(a) + ∫ax q dx
M(x) = M(a) + ∫ax Fs dx  (= area under Fs)

The constants of integration come from the supports: M = 0 at a pin, roller or free end, and Fs at the left end equals the reaction there. A point force is a load squeezed into zero width, so Fs jumps by that force. A couple makes M jump (clockwise on the left of the cut → +).

Each integration raises the degree by one. Largest M: set the derivative to zero, dM/dx = Fs = 0. Same as finding a maximum in calculus.

4 · Chapter 4: from the moment M to the stress σ

  1. Geometry. Plane sections stay plane, so the beam bends into an arc of radius ρ. A fibre at distance y from the neutral layer has length (ρ + y)dθ instead of ρ dθ, so its strain is ε = y/ρ.
  2. Material. Hooke's law: σ = Eε = E·y/ρ. Stress grows linearly with y.
  3. No net axial force: ∫σ dA = (E/ρ)∫y dA = 0. So ∫y dA = 0, which means the neutral axis passes through the centroid. That's why you need centroids.
  4. The stresses add up to M: ∫y·σ dA = (E/ρ)∫y² dA = M. Call Iz = ∫y² dA. Then 1/ρ = M/(EIz).
  5. Put 2 and 4 together:
σ = M·y / Iz   σmax = M / Wz,  Wz = Iz / ymax

Sagging M (+): the bottom is in tension and the top in compression. σ = 0 on the neutral axis.

Moment of inertia Iz (惯性矩)∫y² dAbending stiffness of the shape

Rectangle, worked out: a strip at height y has dA = b·dy, so Iz = ∫−h/2h/2 y²·b dy = b·[y³/3] = bh³/12. Then Wz = (bh³/12)/(h/2) = bh²/6. Circle: I = πd⁴/64, W = πd³/32.

Parallel axis (平行移轴): measure from an axis a away from the centroid: ∫(y + a)² dA = ∫y² dA + 2a∫y dA + a²∫dA. The middle term is 0 (point 3 above), so

Iz = Ic + a²·A

Composite shapes: add the parts (or subtract holes), each moved to the common centroid.

6 · Shear stress, and the procedure

τ = Fs·Sz* / (Iz·b)

Sz* is the static moment of the part of the section beyond the level you're checking, and b is the width at that level. τ is largest at the neutral axis. For a rectangle: τmax = 1.5·Fs/A.

Every beam problem, in order:

  1. Reactions (ΣM about a support, then ΣY to check).
  2. Fs: walk left → right, jumping at forces and sloping under q.
  3. M: add areas under Fs, jumping at couples. Peaks where Fs = 0. M must close to 0 at a free or pinned end.
  4. Ch. 4: take the largest |M| (and check both signs if the section isn't symmetric), then σ = My/Iz.

The lesson

Nine short chapters. They follow the slides (Ch. 1 Introduction, Ch. 2 Geometric composition, start of Ch. 3) but explain each idea in more depth, with examples.

Part 1

Why check the geometry at all?

Until now every problem started with a structure that obviously works, and you only computed forces. Structural mechanics asks an earlier question: will this arrangement of bars, hinges and supports keep its shape under any load?

Left: four bars joined by hinges fold into a parallelogram under the smallest sideways push. Right: one diagonal splits it into two triangles and it can't move.

The left frame is a mechanism (几何可变体系): its shape can change without any bar stretching. It can only stand if the load happens to be perfectly balanced, and the first gust of wind folds it. The right frame is geometrically stable (几何不变体系): the only way to change its shape is to stretch or squash a bar, which the material resists.

Checking the geometry (几何组成分析) does three jobs:

  1. Safety: only a geometrically stable system can be used as a structure.
  2. Choosing the method: it tells you whether equilibrium alone is enough (statically determinate, 静定) or whether you'll also need deformations (statically indeterminate, 超静定).
  3. Order of analysis: the order in which a structure is "assembled" is the reverse of the order in which you calculate it (used for multi-span beams in Part 8).

Throughout this chapter, the bars are treated as rigid: we ignore their small elastic deformations and only ask whether the arrangement can move.

Part 2

Degrees of freedom and constraints

The degree of freedom (DOF, 自由度) of a system is the number of independent ways it can move, i.e. how many numbers you need to describe its position.

  • A point in the plane: 2 DOF (x and y).
  • A rigid piece (刚片), meaning any bar or rigid block, or the ground: 3 DOF (x, y and its rotation θ).

A constraint (约束) removes DOF. The course uses four kinds:

ConstraintRemovesWhy
Chain rod / link (链杆), roller support1stops movement along the link's own line only
Single hinge (单铰), pin support2the two pieces must share a point (x and y), but may rotate
Rigid joint (刚结点), fixed support3shared point and same rotation
Hinge joining n pieces (复铰)2(n − 1)counts as n − 1 single hinges

A pin support is the same as two links to the ground that cross at the pin, which is why the slides sometimes draw it as two short bars.

Worked example Three separate bars have 3 × 3 = 9 DOF. Join all three at one hinge (as on slide p.63): that hinge joins n = 3 pieces, so it counts as 2 single hinges and removes 2 × 2 = 4. DOF = 9 − 4 = 5, which matches the slide's formula n + 2. If instead the three bars are welded together at that point (a rigid joint), it removes 3 × 2 = 6 and DOF = 3: the whole thing moves like one rigid piece.

Redundant constraints (多余约束)

A constraint is redundant if removing it doesn't change the DOF. A point held by two non-parallel links has DOF 0. Add a third link and the DOF is still 0, so one of the three is redundant. Redundant constraints aren't useless (they add strength and safety), but they make the structure statically indeterminate.

Part 3

The counting formula W

Before using the rules, a quick count tells you a lot. For a system of m rigid pieces (not counting the ground), joined by h single hinges and attached to the ground by r support links:

W = 3m − (2h + r)

For a truss (all joints hinged, loads at joints), it's easier to count joints j, bars b and support links r:

W = 2j − (b + r)
ResultWhat it tells you
W > 0Too few constraints: definitely a mechanism. Stop here.
W = 0Exactly enough if they're well arranged. You still need the rules to check the geometry.
W < 0More constraints than needed. If the system is stable, it has −W redundant constraints (statically indeterminate to degree −W).
Worked example (the four beams on slide p.88)
  1. Simple beam: m = 1, h = 0, r = 2 (pin) + 1 (roller) = 3 → W = 3 − 3 = 0.
  2. Fixed end + middle hinge + roller: m = 2, h = 1, r = 3 + 1 = 4 → W = 6 − 2 − 4 = 0.
  3. Fixed at both ends with two middle hinges: m = 3, h = 2, r = 6 → W = 9 − 4 − 6 = −1, one redundant constraint (slide: "DOF 9, constraints 10").
The catch W ≤ 0 is necessary but not sufficient. Three parallel links give W = 0 for a beam, but the beam can still slide sideways. That's why you always follow the count with the rules.
Part 4

The three rules, and the triangle behind them

All three rules come from one fact you know from school geometry: a triangle is fixed by its three side lengths (SSS congruence). If three hinges are joined by three rigid sides, and the hinges aren't in a straight line, the triangle can't change shape. The slides call this the triangle rule (三角形规律).

The three rules are the same triangle, with the sides replaced by links or by rigid pieces.

  1. Rule ①, point + rigid piece: a point joined to a rigid piece by two links not on one line is fixed, with no redundant constraint. Two links joined at a new point are called a binary body (二元体). Adding or removing a binary body never changes whether a system is stable.
  2. Rule ②, two rigid pieces: two pieces joined by one hinge and one link whose line doesn't pass through the hinge, or by three links that are neither concurrent nor all parallel, form one bigger rigid piece with no redundant constraint.
  3. Rule ③, three rigid pieces: three pieces joined pairwise by three hinges not on one line form one bigger rigid piece with no redundant constraint.

The ground counts as a rigid piece. So rule ② with the ground reads: "a body on a pin plus a roller whose line misses the pin is fixed", which is exactly why a simply supported beam works.

Instantaneous (virtual) hinge, 瞬铰 / 虚铰

Two links between the same two pieces act like a hinge located where their lines intersect (slide p.66). For a tiny movement, the piece rotates about that point, just as if there were a real hinge there. This lets you use rules ② and ③ even when there's no physical hinge. The point moves as the links move, which is why it's called "instantaneous". If the two links are parallel, the virtual hinge is at infinity.

Worked example A beam rests on a pin at A and a vertical roller at B. Treat the pin as a hinge to the ground and the roller as one link. Rule ②: one hinge (A) + one link (B), and the link's vertical line through B doesn't pass through A. So beam + ground form one rigid body, with no redundant constraint: stable and statically determinate. If the roller at B were replaced by a horizontal link aimed straight at A, its line would pass through the hinge and the beam could rotate a little about A: unstable.
Part 5

When the rules fail: instantaneously unstable systems

Every rule has a condition ("not on one line", "not concurrent", "not parallel"). Break it and you get an improper arrangement (不恰当约束, slides p.70–71):

  • Three hinges collinear (三铰共线).
  • Three links meeting at one point (三链杆共点): the piece can rotate a little about that point.
  • Three links parallel (三链杆平行): the piece can slide a little sideways (rotation about a point at infinity).

Why does a straight line let it move? Take point B held by two horizontal links of length L, one from each side. Move B down by a small δ. Each link's new length is √(L² + δ²) ≈ L + δ²/(2L). The change in length is proportional to δ², a second-order small quantity, exactly like the dx² you dropped when deriving dM/dx = Fs last week. To first order the links don't stretch at all, so they can't resist the first bit of movement.

Worked example: why it's dangerous Hang P = 1 kN at the middle of two collinear links. B sags until the links make an angle θ with the horizontal. Vertical equilibrium: 2N·sin θ = P, so N = P/(2 sin θ). At θ = 1°, N = 1/(2 × 0.01745) ≈ 28.6 kN, almost 30 times the load. As θ → 0 the force → ∞. An instantaneously unstable system (瞬变体系) develops huge forces and big deflections under a small load, so it is never used as a structure, even though it doesn't collapse outright like a full mechanism (常变体系).
Part 6

Strategy for analysing a whole system

The rules are simple. The skill is choosing which pieces to apply them to. The slides give three approaches (p.73–80):

  1. Start from the ground (从基础出发): take the ground as your first rigid piece and attach things to it one rule at a time, growing a bigger and bigger rigid body.
  2. Remove binary bodies (拆除二元体): peel off any joint held by two links. What remains is simpler, and it has the same stability as the original.
  3. Start from inside (从内部刚片出发): if the system sits on exactly three support links (like a pin + roller), first check the upper part on its own, then attach it to the ground with rule ②.

Choosing pieces (slide p.80): if many supports connect to the ground, make the ground plus its nearby stable bits one big piece. In complicated systems, choose pieces that are "evenly spread out", each joined to the others by hinges or pairs of links.

Worked example: writing it up the way the course expects

System: beam ABC on a pin at A and a roller at B, with a second beam CD hinged to it at C and resting on a roller at D.

分析 Analysis. (1) ABC + ground: hinge A + link B, whose line misses A → rule ②, one rigid body, no redundant constraint. (2) CD + that body: hinge C + link D, whose line misses C → rule ② again.

结论 Conclusion. Geometrically stable with no redundant constraints, so it is statically determinate. Count check: m = 2, h = 1, r = 2 + 1 + 1 = 4 → W = 6 − 2 − 4 = 0 ✓.

Part 7

Geometry decides static determinacy

Each rigid piece gives you 3 equilibrium equations (ΣX, ΣY, ΣM). Each constraint brings one unknown force. So:

  • Statically determinate (静定): stable with no redundant constraints. Unknowns = equations, and equilibrium alone gives every reaction and internal force.
  • Statically indeterminate (超静定): stable with redundant constraints. There are more unknowns than equations, and the degree of indeterminacy is the number of redundant constraints.
You've met this before Week 4 homework: a steel rod fixed at both ends with loads in the middle. One rigid piece (3 equations) with 6 constraints gives 3 redundant constraints. For axial loading only, one equation (ΣX) and two unknown end forces leave one equation short. That's why the hint said "use the deformation condition": the extra equation came from the total change in length being zero. Statically indeterminate always means you need deformation.

Slide p.88's last example: m = 2 pieces (DOF 6) with 10 constraints, so it's 4 times statically indeterminate (if stable).

Part 8

Statically determinate multi-span beams (静定多跨梁)

A long bridge often uses a chain of beams joined by hinges (slide p.92, a Gerber beam). Geometric analysis shows how the chain is assembled:

  • The basic part (基本部分) is stable on its own supports, for example a beam on a pin and a roller, or a cantilever.
  • An attached part (附属部分) needs the basic part to stand: it has a hinge into the basic part and only one support of its own.

How force flows: a load on an attached part travels through the hinge into the basic part. A load on the basic part does not reach the attached part. So you analyse in the reverse order of assembly (slide p.94: 装 assemble ← / 拆 take apart →): attached parts first, then pass the hinge force (equal and opposite) to the part underneath. A hinge transmits force but no moment, so M = 0 at every hinge, which makes a great check.

Worked example

AB is a 2 m cantilever fixed at A. BC is 4 m long, hinged to it at B and resting on a roller at C. q = 5 kN/m acts over the whole 6 m.

  1. Assembly: AB (fixed) is the basic part. BC (hinge B + roller C) is attached.
  2. BC first (a simple beam of span 4): RC = q·4/2 = 10 kN, and the hinge holds BC up with 10 kN.
  3. AB next: BC pushes down on the cantilever tip with 10 kN. Load on AB: 5 × 2 = 10 kN spread + 10 kN at the tip. RA = 20 kN, MA = −(10 × 2 + 5 × 2²/2) = −30 kN·m (hogging).
  4. Checks: RA + RC = 30 = 5 × 6 ✓. MB = 0 at the hinge ✓. The peak in BC is ql²/8 = 10 kN·m at its middle.
Part 9

Stability of a slender column (稳定 / 临界力)

"Stability" now means something else: is the equilibrium stable? Think of a ball. In a bowl, push it and it rolls back (stable). On top of a dome, it rolls away (unstable). A straight column under compression is like that ball: below a certain load, a small sideways push dies away; above it, the column bends further and further. That load is the critical force Fcr.

The spring model (slide p.26)

A rigid bar of length l, pinned at the base, held at the top by a horizontal spring of stiffness k, carries P on top. Tip it sideways by a small Δ. The spring pulls back with kΔ, which gives a restoring moment kΔ·l about the base. The load, now offset by Δ, gives an overturning moment P·Δ. It returns if kΔl > PΔ, i.e. P < kl. The two balance exactly when kΔl − PΔ = 0, so Pcr = k·l. Notice that Δ cancels: the critical load doesn't depend on how hard you push.

Euler's formula (slide p.27)

A real column is its own spring: bending stiffness EI resists the bending. Solving the bending equation (EI·v″ = −P·v, a sine-wave solution) gives:

Fcr = π²EI / (μl)²

The buckled shapes. μl is the length of one "half sine wave" in each shape: the effective length.

  • E: the material (steel 200 GPa vs timber about 10 GPa).
  • I: the section. Use the smallest I, because the column buckles about its weakest axis.
  • μ: the end conditions. μ = 1 (pinned–pinned), 2 (fixed–free), 0.7 (fixed–pinned), 0.5 (fixed–fixed, the top free only to move vertically).
  • (μl)² is in the denominator: double the length and Fcr drops to a quarter.
Worked example Steel (E = 200 GPa = 200 000 MPa) circular column, d = 50 mm, pinned at both ends, l = 3 m. I = πd⁴/64 = π × 50⁴/64 ≈ 306 800 mm⁴. Fcr = π² × 200 000 × 306 800 / 3000² ≈ 67 300 N = 67.3 kN. Make it a flagpole (fixed at the bottom, free at the top, μ = 2): (μl)² is 4× bigger, so Fcr ≈ 16.8 kN.
